1945–1953

Truman

Eight years, three FRUS volumes, and the founding of everything that followed — from a diplomatic mission's status, to Independence, to the actual accession of Kashmir, to the policy paper that first set a plebiscite as the American goal.

The relationship that eventually inherited the Kashmir dispute did not begin with America stepping into a British vacuum. It began with Britain proposing closer diplomatic ties in January 1946, and Washington saying not yet.

7 January 1946A British proposal Washington wants to postpone

Secretary of State Byrnes cabled Commissioner George Merrell in New Delhi: Britain, through its embassy in Washington, had asked the State Department to reconsider raising India's diplomatic representation to full ministerial rank. Washington's answer was no — or not yet.

Full text read
Byrnes tells Merrell the US prefers to wait ▾

"Dept has informed BritEmb that... it prefers to postpone making any decision in this matter." The request had, in fact, first come from Britain the previous October — Washington had been stalling for months.

Secretary of State to Commissioner in India, 7 January 1946. FRUS 1946, Vol. V, Document 42.

Merrell's own reply, two days later, revealed something about how the relationship had actually worked until then: "We have never endeavored to conceal the fact that HMG's attitude has been responsible for our 'quasi-diplomatic' status." It was Britain, not Washington, that had kept the US-India relationship formally low-key.

February 1946The Cabinet Mission, and a mutiny

On 19 February, Attlee announced a Cabinet Mission to negotiate India's constitutional future. Two days later, a senior British official told Ambassador Winant privately that Jinnah's Pakistan demand had "gained such momentum that doubtful if Jinnah or anyone else could apply the brakes." Within days, the Royal Indian Navy mutinied in Bombay and spread to Karachi. British officials speaking to the Americans afterward went further than any public statement: Pethick-Lawrence, on India's future, said Britain wanted to build "an India for Indians but not an India for some other power" — Cold War language, deployed a year before the term existed.

April 1946A live intelligence dispute, addressed to Henderson

The mutiny's fallout produced America's own internal disagreement. Merrell found no evidence Communists had instigated the resulting riots — only exploited them. The consulates in Karachi and Madras reported the opposite, with "undoubted evidence of organization — probably of Communistic origin." The memo laying out the dispute went to Loy Henderson, then a desk officer — the same man who would arrive as Ambassador two and a half years later, mid-crisis.

June 1946Merrell explains why India distrusts America too

During a famine crisis, Merrell reported that Indian press hostility toward the US wasn't really about British colonialism — it was about broken American wartime promises. "During the early part of our participation in the recent war, our Government... 'oversold' itself to Indians," creating hopes of liberation that were never realized. He traced it back further still, to Vallabhbhai Patel, who told an American officer that Indians' disillusionment first began with Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points, a generation earlier.

September–October 1946Truman decides

The Interim Government took office 2 September, representative of "at least 80 percent" of Indians since the Muslim League still refused to join. A memo dated 30 August carries Truman's own handwritten approval of the mission-status upgrade, given 3 September. By October, the two countries agreed to exchange ambassadors, and Merrell became Chargé d'Affaires pending a full appointment.

December 1946The crisis

The Cabinet Mission Plan collapsed over a single technical question — separate provincial votes versus majority rule on regional groupings. On 7 December, Britain told Washington it "believes it might be helpful" if America informally pressed Nehru — and four days later, Acheson instructed Merrell to deny any British involvement while delivering exactly that British-requested message.

Full text read
Washington tells its own envoy to deny British involvement — while acting on it ▾

"Important dispel any Indian belief US actions inspired at instance Brit," Acheson cabled, instructing Merrell to stress the approach was "made solely on US initiative." Three days earlier, that approach had in fact originated from a British request.

Acting Secretary of State to Chargé Merrell, 11 December 1946. FRUS 1946, Vol. V, Document 64.

Merrell delivered the message to Patel, and got the sharpest response any American diplomat received all year — an accusation that the US "favored the strong," and a pointed historical parallel: "In course of American revolution there was period when fighting became necessary." That same evening, Rajagopalachari told Merrell the Cabinet Mission Plan's "ten year experiment" clause had originally been an American idea, not British. Three days later, Nehru offered Merrell an unprompted personal aside comparing himself to Jinnah's "Hindu background." By month's end, Liaquat Ali Khan admitted the League was "beginning to feel that perhaps outright Pakistan would be only means" of securing its goals.

1947Independence, and the Maharaja named before the crisis

Henry Grady presented credentials as first US Ambassador on 25 June 1947. Nehru's first request of him: help persuade Britain to keep Mountbatten on as Governor-General of both new dominions, not just India. Independence came 15 August. By 7 October, with Partition violence dominating every exchange, India's Ambassador Asaf Ali met Under Secretary Lovett — and mentioned, almost in passing, that India's "northernmost boundary lay only fifteen miles from Soviet territory," a State Department footnote clarifying this was "a boundary of the domains of the Maharaja of Kashmir who has not yet acceded to India or Pakistan." Fifteen days later, that undecided status stopped being a footnote.

Full text read — the founding document
The accession itself, reported the day after it happened ▾

"The Maharaja... signed instrument of accession which was accepted same day by Mountbatten." Mountbatten's own acceptance letter is quoted directly: "as soon as law and order have been restored in Kashmir and her soil cleared of the invader the question of the state's accession should be settled by reference to the people." A footnote corrects the body text's own date error — signed 26 October, not 27th, as the cable claimed.

The Chargé in Pakistan (Lewis) to the Secretary of State, 28 October 1947. FRUS 1947, Vol. III, Document 119.

Two weeks later, Grady's own weary verdict on a refugee-airlift request: "The roots of the conflict which brought about the division of India too deep to be healed or particularly ameliorated by loan of ten planes."

1 November – 29 December 1947Where the plebiscite idea actually came from, and the Lahore talks: a near-miss, then a collapse

The plebiscite that shaped everything downstream of this archive is usually read as something the UN, or the US, or Britain imposed on the dispute. The record says otherwise: Nehru proposed it himself, in public, before any of them were involved.

Full text read
Nehru's own words, quoted in Washington's first position paper — and a startlingly condescending line beside them ▾

The State Department's UN position paper quotes Nehru's 1 November speech directly: "as soon as Kashmir is free from the invaders our troops will have no further necessity to remain there and the fate of Kashmir will be left in the hands of the people of Kashmir" — Nehru himself suggesting a referendum "under international auspices like the United Nations." The same paper, discussing who could credibly assess Kashmiri opinion, states plainly that "few persons other than British political agents and missionaries have first hand knowledge of the people of Kashmir," and that "the people of Kashmir are largely illiterate and without political consciousness" — a real, documented instance of how condescendingly Washington privately regarded the population whose fate the paper otherwise treats with genuine care.

The Acting Secretary of State to the Embassy in India, enclosing the UN position paper, 2 December 1947. FRUS 1947, Vol. III, Document 121 (501.BB/12–247).

Two days after Grady reported Nehru's willingness to meet Jinnah at Lahore, the near-miss described below happened — but the paper above shows the plebiscite framing already existed in Nehru's own words two days earlier still.

Full text read
Nehru was willing to go to Lahore — until Jinnah accused India of "fraud" ▾

Bajpai told Grady that Mountbatten had been trying to arrange a conference of the two Governors-General and Prime Ministers at Lahore. Jinnah told Mountbatten he "had no confidence in Government of India." Despite this, "Nehru was prepared to go to Lahore until he read Jinnah's statement accusing GOI of 'fraud' in connection with accession of Kashmir." Jinnah's own opening proposal — simultaneous troop withdrawal — India read as itself proof of Pakistani control over the "invaders," who Bajpai said had "looted and destroyed at least 100 villages" on the border.

Ambassador Grady to the Secretary of State, 3 November 1947. FRUS 1947, Vol. III, Document 120 (845.00/11–347).

The conference happened anyway, weeks later. Before it collapsed, Washington itself was quietly approached about an entirely different plebiscite mechanism — one that would have kept every Great Power, including the Soviet Union, off the commission entirely.

Full text read
Pandit's proposal: a plebiscite commission with no Great Powers at all ▾

A month before the formal UN referral, Nehru's sister and India's UN delegate, Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit, approached State Department officials in New York "at instance Nehru," proposing a plebiscite under adult suffrage the following spring, supervised by "small and disinterested countries" — with the Great Powers, including the USSR, explicitly excluded from the commission. Pakistan's Ambassador Ispahani, sounded out separately, "seemed favorably disposed" — a word chosen with care: the cable's own footnote records the draft originally said he "was" favorably disposed, hand-corrected to "seemed" before sending. Lovett's own closing worry reads almost prophetic: that India going to the UN "without some such prearranged plan might lead to unnecessary complications and crystallize pattern GOIGOP hostility."

The Acting Secretary of State to the Embassy in India, 26 December 1947. FRUS 1947, Vol. III, Document 123 (745.45F/12–2447).

Two days earlier, Bajpai had already told Donovan how India meant to handle the coming UN case — and revealed a specific mistrust that shaped it.

Full text read
"GOI will not permit adjudication by UK or member British Commonwealth" ▾

Bajpai told Donovan that if no satisfactory reply came within a week, India would take the matter to the Security Council — and was explicit that India "will not permit adjudication by UK or member British Commonwealth." He had already briefed the French Ambassador and the UK High Commissioner, but hadn't decided whether to inform Australia. Bajpai conceded Pakistan "might not be able to stop all assistance to raiders," but insisted Pakistan had "done absolutely nothing to check" them.

The Chargé in India (Donovan) to the Secretary of State, 24 December 1947. FRUS 1947, Vol. III, Document 122 (745.45F/12–2447).

By late December, the Lahore conference itself had broken down entirely.

Full text read
Why Lahore failed: Pakistan's fear that Abdullah would jail the opposition and rig the outcome ▾

Bajpai explained the breakdown directly to Donovan: the sticking point was who would administer Kashmir until a plebiscite. Specifically, "Pakistan felt if Sheikh Abdullah continued as head of emergency administration he would be able, after imprisoning opposition raiders, to obtain majority in favor of the administration and in favor of approval of accession of Kashmir to India." On why India was turning to the UN rather than continuing bilateral talks, Bajpai argued Pakistan's raiders were "stateless persons" since Islamabad refused responsibility for them, making direct negotiation under UN Charter Article 33 "impracticable." Most strikingly: "unless Pakistan took measures restrain entry raiders... GOI troops might be compelled enter Pakistan" — a threat Donovan's own cable judged "could only result open hostilities between GOI and GOP."

The Chargé in India (Donovan) to the Secretary of State, 29 December 1947. FRUS 1947, Vol. III, Document 125 (745.45F/12–2947).

Read together, these five documents settle a question the archive had left open, and correct a simpler version of the story that stood here before: India's UN referral wasn't a first move, and the plebiscite wasn't handed down by outside powers — it followed a genuine, personal attempt at direct settlement between Nehru and Jinnah that failed specifically over who would control the vote, with India's own government privately viewing war as the only alternative to internationalizing the dispute, and quietly working to exclude Britain from adjudicating it.

Full text read
Liaquat's reply to Nehru: Kashmir isn't the dispute, it's a symptom of one ▾

Liaquat Ali Khan's written reply to Nehru, six typewritten pages, went well beyond Kashmir itself: it developed "point by point" the thesis that "Hindus accepted partition in bad faith," that "Pakistan's very existence is the chief casus belli," and that India had been "following calculated pattern to annoy, weaken and destroy Pakistan" — with Kashmir framed as "but one incident in long chain such events." Read against everything else in this section, it shows how quickly the dispute had already been absorbed into a much larger, more existential mutual suspicion on Pakistan's side, barely two months after the accession.

Referenced in the Chargé in Pakistan (Lewis) to the Secretary of State, 3 January 1948, summarising Liaquat's letter of 30 December 1947. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 209.
Full text read
Nehru, days before the UN case opens: west Punjab is now "tribal territory" — and India claims a right to strike into Pakistan ▾

Days after conveying Washington's concern to Nehru directly, Donovan reported Nehru's own private read on the security situation: "larger part of west Punjab was now for all practical purposes 'tribal territory'" that Pakistan's government could "neither disarm nor control." Nehru assured Donovan India's troops would not take offensive action against Pakistan, but said he feared Kashmiri irregulars might retaliate with raids of their own into west Punjab. Days later, India's External Affairs Department put something bolder in writing: in a formal note to the Embassy, it stated India "would have been within their rights in entering Pakistan territory in order to strike at bases" used by the raiders — a direct claim to a cross-border strike right, on the record, weeks into the UN process.

The Chargé in India (Donovan) to the Secretary of State, 3 January 1948 (FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 211) and 7 January 1948 (Document 213).

6 January 1948Marshall's founding policy paper

The day the UN Security Council first convened on Kashmir, Secretary of State George Marshall signed the paper defining American strategy. It reveals that the plebiscite outcome, so associated with the UN's later resolutions, was America's own preferred solution from the start.

Full text read — the founding document
Marshall: a plebiscite is the only durable answer — and let Britain lead ▾

"It is the opinion of the Department that the only solution acceptable to all parties concerned in the Kashmir problem will eventually be a determination, probably by plebiscite... taking into account the possibility that some form of partition may be proposed." Washington wanted Britain "as the most directly concerned third govt" to lead, positioning the US as "virtually inevitable second choice" — while noting British and American objectives were already closely aligned. The partition clause is easy to miss on a first read, but it means the October 1948 partition discussion below wasn't a later, secret departure from this paper — it was a contingency built into American policy from day one.

The Secretary of State to Austin, 6 January 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 212.

Four days before Britain formalized the partition-adjacent plan already logged here, a British official used a word no one else in this archive reaches for.

Full text read
"The danger of a holocaust on the Indian subcontinent" ▾

Philip Noel-Baker opened the conversation "emphasizing the danger of a holocaust on the Indian subcontinent arising from the dispute between India and Pakistan over Kashmir. Neither side can back down," he said, arguing "only the voice of international authority can prevent war." He and Lord Ismay then laid out the substance of what became, four days later, the formal British plan already recorded here — informal conciliation, a stronger Council warning, and UN administration of Kashmir pending a plebiscite.

Memorandum of Conversation, 10 January 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 215.

Nine days later, Austin's own meeting with Zafrullah Khan and Ispahani revealed the exact fault line that would collapse the Lahore talks eleven months later — already fully formed in January.

Full text read
The Abdullah fight, present from the very first month ▾

Zafrullah laid out two sticking points to Austin directly: whether military and political settlement should be agreed simultaneously (Pakistan's position) or security first, politics later under a UN commission (India's position) — and "the kind of interim government." Pakistan wanted "an impartial head" and was "opposed to Sheik Abdullah"; India "stands for Sheik Abdullah." This is the identical dispute that would sink the Lahore conference in December — already fully articulated in the dispute's third week.

The US Representative at the UN (Austin) to the Secretary of State, 19 January 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 221 (501.BC/1–1948).

Eight days later, Britain floated a concrete partition plan — Pakistani troops north, Indian troops south, the Valley jointly held under a neutral UN commander — that never went anywhere. Six weeks after that, Sheikh Abdullah told Grady he doubted either accession outcome would ever be accepted, and proposed instead that Kashmir stay internally independent with defense run jointly by both dominions.

February–March 1948Washington wasn't of one mind, and neither was the Council

The record often reads as if American strategy moved as a single coordinated block. It didn't. Within weeks of Marshall's paper, Washington's own two ambassadors were pulling in different directions, and the Council itself was riven by procedural mistrust between India and Pakistan's delegations.

Full text read
Grady and Austin, contradicting each other ▾

USUN's Noyes reported with alarm that Ambassador Grady in New Delhi had, in a 7 February telegram, ended up "giving his support to a procedure which is almost exactly like the original Indian position" — directly at odds with Ambassador Austin at the UN, who was "rather far out in front" on a tougher interim-administration stance. Noyes asked Washington to "straighten New Delhi out." Grady's own proposal, separately, suggested the plebiscite be preceded by parliamentary elections — nearly identical to India's own 27 January scheme, which specified those elections would happen "under interim government headed by Sheikh Abdullah."

Memorandum of Conversation by Thurston, 9 February 1948 (Document 232); Secretary of State to Embassy in India, 7 February 1948 (Document 234). FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1.

Six weeks later, Pakistan's Foreign Minister Zafrullah Khan complained bitterly about being outmaneuvered at the Council itself — China's delegate, Tsiang, had privately discussed a Plebiscite Administrator concept with him over lunch on 11 March, then presented a formal draft resolution built on that conversation a week later, without the customary simultaneous notice to both parties that previous Council presidents had observed. Zafrullah suspected the web ran wider than Tsiang alone: Tsiang himself said, in front of Zafrullah, that one provision had been "put in at the suggestion of Noel-Baker"; Zafrullah had separately discussed the same ideas with Attlee in London, and suspected Mountbatten's hand in it too. It was a small procedural grievance on its face, but it fed a larger Pakistani suspicion that the process itself wasn't neutral — Britain, China and India all appearing, from Karachi's vantage point, to be coordinating around him.

8 May 1948Regular troops, confirmed — before either side admitted it

Pakistan's public position throughout this period was that only irregular tribesmen were fighting in Kashmir. American intelligence reaching Washington that spring told a different story.

Full text read
Three Pakistani Army battalions, and anti-aircraft guns firing on Indian planes ▾

"Pakistan has three regular Pakistan Army battalions Kashmir now, one vicinity Uri, one vicinity Poonch, one vicinity Mirpur... Two ack-ack guns on Pakistan side Kohala bridge have instructions fire and have fired at Indian planes attempting bomb bridge." The report goes on to assess how an Indian drive toward Mirpur could threaten canal headwaters feeding western Punjab's irrigation system — real operational, strategic detail, not diplomatic language.

Karachi to the Secretary of State, 8 May 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 268 (745.45F/5–848).

By July, the regular-troops issue had gone public — India was using it to harden its own negotiating position, and internal Indian government debate over the parallel Hyderabad crisis was surfacing too. Marshall himself, five days earlier, had already signed off on a much starker warning.

Full text read
Marshall's five-point war warning ▾

Britain feared India and Pakistan were "near open hostilities," citing five specific factors relayed by Marshall to Huddle: confirmed regular Pakistani troops already clashing with Indian forces; India improving its military position before UNCIP's arrival; India's belief Pakistan was planning an air attack on Indian bases in Kashmir; the possibility of Indian military action against Hyderabad; and fear the Royal Indian Air Force might intercept aircraft allegedly running arms between Pakistan and Hyderabad. Britain wanted the Commission to move into Kashmir immediately, without "protracted discussions" in Delhi or Karachi first — and Marshall agreed.

The Secretary of State to the US Representative on UNCIP (Huddle), 14 July 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 277 (501.BC Kashmir/7–1448).
Full text read
Regular troops go public, and a minister admits Hyderabad may need a military solution ▾

"Presence regular GOP troops in Kashmir, widely publicized by GOI in recent weeks, has strengthened GOI determination demand withdrawal raiders and GOP troops before accepting cease-fire order," Donovan reported — the private American finding from May had become India's own public negotiating leverage by July. On the separate Hyderabad crisis, V.P. Menon, Secretary of India's States Ministry, told an embassy officer privately that "although he personally was convinced military solution would be necessary," his government still hoped for a peaceful settlement — the same gap between private assessment and public position found throughout this archive on Kashmir itself.

The Chargé in India (Donovan) to the Secretary of State, 19 July 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 279 (745.45F/7–1948).

Two days later, UNCIP's own chairman confirmed the scale had grown well past battalions — and that India still hadn't been told.

Full text read
Three full brigades, admitted by Pakistan to UNCIP directly — with India not yet informed ▾

"UN commission India–Pakistan during official conversations in Karachi was informed that three brigades Pakistan troops are inside Kashmir frontiers. India has not been notified of this." The cable also records that "competent Indian officials indicate plebiscite this year impracticable," while Pakistani officials at that point still favored a plebiscite without reservation on timing — the two governments' actual private assessments already diverging from what either would say in public.

The Chargé in India (Donovan), transmitting a message from the Chairman of UNCIP, to the Secretary of State, 21 July 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 281 (501.BC Kashmir/7–2148).

6 September – 1 November 1948The Commission concedes failure, and partition emerges as the real alternative

The August 13 ceasefire resolution looked, on paper, like the moment the war finally found its off-ramp. Within weeks, both sides' actual responses showed why it wouldn't hold.

Full text read
Pakistan's "acceptance" that wasn't — and a government that couldn't bind its own side ▾

Pakistan's 6 September reply nominally accepted the resolution, but attached a condition so sweeping that Huddle's own assessment was blunt: "Effect, therefore, of Pakistan decision is nonacceptance Commission's August 13 proposals." Buried in the same letter was something more structurally serious: Pakistan's Foreign Minister stated his government's views were "not binding on Azad Kashmir Government nor do they reflect Azad Kashmir views" — directly contradicting assurances Pakistan had previously given the Commission, and revealing that Islamabad could not simply speak for the territory and forces it controlled.

The US Representative on UNCIP (Huddle) to the Secretary of State, 6 September 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 302 (501.BC Kashmir/9–648).

By 10 September, the Commission that had drafted the resolution privately conceded it was already dead.

Full text read
"Foredoomed to failure": the Commission's own resolution quietly abandoned ▾

By 10 September, UNCIP's own communiqué stated that "immediate effectuation of its proposal of August 13 is not to be envisaged." The US Chargé in Pakistan offered a diagnosis that reads almost as an epitaph for the whole mediation effort: "It was perhaps a foregone conclusion that however impartial, sincere and understanding the members of the Commission might be their mission was foredoomed to failure because any concessions which the Commission might make to meet the point of view of the Government of India would be unacceptable to the Government of Pakistan and vice versa." The same dispatch names the specific grievance: a Pakistani official directly challenged Nehru's own August 20 acceptance letter, which stated that if a plebiscite were held, "Pakistan shall have no part in the organization and conduct of the plebiscite or in any other matter of internal administration in the State" — asking pointedly, "Is it honestly to be expected that Pakistan could accept such a condition?" Two weeks later, the Commission left the subcontinent for Geneva, asking both governments to simply "lessen the existing tension" while it was away.

The Chargé in Pakistan (Lewis) to the Secretary of State, 10 September 1948 (Document 304); further correspondence, 24 September 1948 (Document 325). FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1.

By late September, informal backchannel talks in Paris were trying to salvage the resolution, and Washington was quietly naming the psychology driving Pakistan's resistance.

Full text read
"Deep-seated fear Indian aggression" — and doubt that India's own acceptance was genuine ▾

Washington judged that "GOP attitude conditioned by deep-seated fear Indian aggression," which drove Pakistan's insistence on safeguards after any troop withdrawal. But the same cable records the State Department's own doubt that India's response to the Commission's proposals was "fully compatible" with the resolution it had nominally accepted — a crack in India's position mirroring the one already known in Pakistan's.

The Acting Secretary of State to the US Representative on UNCIP (Huddle), 29 September 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 327 (501.BC Kashmir/9–2948).

By early October, the doubt had reached the very top of the State Department — not doubt about which settlement to pursue, but doubt about whether the UN process itself was worth continuing at all.

Full text read
Lovett himself weighs abandoning the UN process entirely ▾

Huddle had suggested UNCIP could "accomplish little at this stage." Lovett, signing personally, laid out the real alternative under consideration: that "negotiations should revert to bilateral talks without any further UN action." He didn't decide it outright — the cable ends by asking Huddle to report urgently on UNCIP's actual progress — but the fact a Under Secretary of State was weighing abandoning the entire UN mechanism, just days before the partition discussion below, is the missing context for why partition suddenly became worth discussing.

The Acting Secretary of State (Lovett) to Huddle, at Geneva, 7 October 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 339 (501.BC Kashmir/10–548).

Four days later, the partition secret already logged here began — and it now reads less like a stray idea and more like the direct product of this crisis of confidence.

Full text read
The first signal: "worthy careful and serious consideration" ▾

Nineteen days before Austin's better-known telegram, Washington told its own UNCIP representative the same thing in almost identical words: "Re possibility combining scheme plebiscite with outright partition certain areas Jammu and Kashmir between GOI and GOP, Dept has for some time believed such a formula worthy careful and serious consideration though for reasons probably similar to Brit we have so far not been disposed take initiative." The cable is explicit about the coordination machinery behind it — "close US–UK collaboration which has prevailed during UN consideration Kashmir question" — and asks Huddle to find out whether Britain had a specific partition plan in mind before Washington moved at all.

The Acting Secretary of State to the US Representative on UNCIP (Huddle), 11 October 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 342 (501.BC Kashmir/10–1148).
Full text read
"We have for some time considered that such a formula is worthy of careful consideration" ▾

"Looking now to the possibility of combining the principle of a plebiscite with partition of certain areas of Jammu and Kashmir, we have for some time considered that such a formula is worthy of careful consideration. For reasons that are obvious we have, however, been disinclined to take the initiative on the point." Austin reports UNCIP itself found the idea unpopular, "especially with the Moslems of both parties who feared that by any partition they would lose the Kashmir Valley" — but that some Commission members still saw "distinct administrative advantages" to partition in certain areas. The Commission's interim report, Austin adds, was being built in two parts: Part 1 "largely historical," with any actual plebiscite recommendations held back for Part 2 — and an informal 28 October Paris meeting had already been abandoned at the suggestion of the UN Secretary-General and the American commissioner, in favor of more time to consult with Britain first.

The US Representative at the UN (Austin) to the Secretary of State, 30 October 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 353 (501.BC Kashmir/10–3048).

Two days later, the secret stopped being purely American. A UNCIP commissioner reported that Pakistan itself had put a specific version of partition on the table — and revealed something genuinely unexpected about the two men on opposite sides of the war.

Full text read
Pakistan's own partition proposal — and a coalition even Sheikh Abdullah might not have opposed ▾

Pakistan had put two options on the table: unconditional acceptance of the August 13 resolution, or "partition of Jammu-Kashmir on the lines now existing" — the territory already held by Azad Kashmir and Pakistan on one side, the territory held by India and Sheikh Abdullah's government on the other, formalizing the actual battle lines rather than the disputed accession. Liaquat Ali Khan had told Nehru Pakistan accepted the August resolution, but only if India first accepted the April plebiscite terms — a condition, the commissioner notes, that "conflicts with statements made by both Sheikh Abdullah and Ghulam Abbas" separately. Pakistani officials also floated a "neutralized" coalition government for the plebiscite period, fusing the Azad Kashmir movement with Sheikh Abdullah's own administration — and Commission members had learned, through informal conversations with both men, that Abdullah and Ghulam Abbas "might not be adverse to forming a coalition."

A member of UNCIP, "Developments on the Kashmir Problem," Paris, 1 November 1948, transmitted in the Commission's despatch No. 10 of the same date. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 354.

Read as a sequence rather than as isolated cables, this is a clean chain of causation: the Commission privately conceding its own resolution was dead by mid-September, Washington questioning whether the whole UN process was worth continuing by early October, the partition secret emerging four days later, and Pakistan independently proposing a version of the same thing within the fortnight — all before Henderson had even landed in New Delhi.

19–20 November 1948Henderson arrives, into live fighting

Loy Henderson — who had read the RIN Mutiny intelligence dispute as a desk officer two and a half years earlier — took charge as Ambassador on 19 November 1948. The next day, a military appreciation crossed his desk: India's capture of Dras, and a note that "a possible advance toward Kargil should not be overlooked." Fifty-one years before Kargil became globally known, it appears here as a tactical footnote in Henderson's first briefing. Two days later, in Paris, John Foster Dulles briefly inherited the Kashmir file too — not through any particular interest of his own, but purely by institutional succession, becoming Acting Chairman of the US UN delegation "upon the departure of Secretary Marshall," with Austin having already returned to Washington ill. Dulles's Kashmir involvement, in other words, was a few weeks of procedural inheritance five years before he became Secretary of State himself — worth noting precisely so as not to overstate it.

22 November – 27 December 1948The ceasefire's difficult birth

Getting from Pakistan's qualified non-acceptance in September to an actual ceasefire on New Year's Day took another five weeks of granular negotiation — including a dispute over whether the Commission's own report to the Security Council was even accurate.

Full text read
UNCIP appeals to both sides, and admits it needs its own senior military adviser ▾

The Commission formally appealed to both governments to "refrain from any action which might aggravate the military and political situation" — and separately asked the UN Secretary-General, "as a matter of urgency," to appoint a military adviser "with combat experience and rank brigadier or higher." UNCIP's own military expertise, five months into overseeing a war, was still being assembled.

The US Representative on UNCIP (Huddle) to the Secretary of State, 22 November 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 366 (501.BC Kashmir/11–2248).
Full text read
A dispute over whether UNCIP's own report was honest, and a new idea: settle Kashmir by prewar census ▾

UNCIP's draft report to the Security Council stated India had "signified their acceptance of the resolution as a whole" — but Pakistani officials protested this was misleading, since Nehru's actual August letter had attached conditions, "at least two which conditions were vital Pakistan." The US Chargé personally argued the discrepancy needed sorting out, since "elemental justice requires a thorough understanding" of it before the Security Council reacted. The same cable records Pakistan quietly floating something not seen elsewhere in this record: given the "obvious difficulties" of actually holding a vote, settling Kashmir's fate using pre-war census returns instead of a live plebiscite.

The Chargé in Pakistan (Lewis) to the Secretary of State, 26 November 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 371 (501.BC Kashmir/11–2648).

Three weeks later, the negotiations had narrowed to specific, granular objections — and revealed that the plebiscite's own governing framework contained a built-in exit.

Full text read
The plebiscite's own escape clause: "both parties agree that other means offer more practical solution" ▾

UNCIP commissioner Lozano told Henderson that Nehru had raised procedural objections to the December 11 proposals — chiefly the timing of appointing a Plebiscite Administrator. Lozano's own account of the safeguards is the important part: a plebiscite would follow the December 11 terms unless "(1) PA should find it technically impracticable; (2) one or both parties fail to act in good faith or (3) both parties agree that other means offer more practical solution." That third clause is the same door the partition and coalition ideas already in this archive were walking through — not an outside deviation from the plan, but something the plan itself permitted. Lozano also admitted worrying that India, once the ceasefire and withdrawal stages were complete, "might seek avoid plebiscite and reopen whole problem." Both he and Bajpai were, at this point, "fairly optimistic."

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, 21 December 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 387 (501.BC Kashmir/12–2148).

Pakistan formally accepted six days later — and four days after that, the ceasefire already logged in this archive took effect at midnight on New Year's Day.

Cited, not read
Pakistan's formal acceptance, closing the loop ▾

Confirmed to exist by title — "Kashmir Dispute: Acceptance by Pakistan of UNCIP Proposals" — but not yet opened and read in full.

The Chargé in Pakistan (Lewis) to the Secretary of State, 27 December 1948. FRUS 1948, Vol. V, Part 1, Document 390 (501.BC Kashmir/12–2748).

31 December 1948Nehru's own words, as the ceasefire takes effect

Not a US document — Nehru's private New Year's Eve letter to Mountbatten, written the night the ceasefire he'd just ordered was taking hold, fifteen months into the fighting.

Full text read — not a US document
"It is a great thing to have a ceasefire and to put an end to the 15 months of fighting" ▾

"We felt however that in view of the general agreement arrived at, it would be a senseless waste of life not to have an immediate ceasefire... We have suggested 11:59 hours on January 1st for the ceasefire... The future in regard to Kashmir is full of difficulty and there is going to be a great deal of trouble. But it is a great thing to have a ceasefire." He closes: "As I was dictating this letter, the New Year has crept in."

Nehru to Lord Mountbatten, 31 December 1948. Selected Works of Jawaharlal Nehru, Second Series, Vol. 9, pp. 227–228 (The Nehru Archive).

1–4 January 1949The ceasefire, formalized — with American boots on the ground

The ceasefire took effect at midnight, 1 January 1949. Within days, Washington confirmed something not widely appreciated: American personnel themselves became part of enforcing it.

Full text read
The US is asked to supply 20 observer officers ▾

"The Governments of both countries... have already voluntarily put the cease fire agreement into effect as of 12 o'clock midnight January 1, 1949," the Acting Secretary of State wrote to the Secretary of Defense — and confirmed the UN Secretary-General had formally asked the United States to furnish twenty officers to observe it, with the Commission expected back on the subcontinent before 20 January.

The Acting Secretary of State to the Secretary of Defense (Forrestal), 4 January 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, 501.BC Kashmir/1–449.

28 April 1949A second, secret Karachi Agreement

Not a US document, and not part of the same negotiation this archive otherwise tracks — but worth flagging clearly, since the naming collision could mislead future research. Three months before the well-known July 1949 India-Pakistan military Karachi Agreement (which fixed the ceasefire line and is already recorded above), a wholly separate agreement was reportedly signed in Karachi between Pakistan's government and the Azad Kashmir administration itself.

Secondary — not a US document, not independently verified here
Azad Kashmir cedes Gilgit-Baltistan to Pakistan — and it stayed secret for over 40 years ▾

Signed by Mushtaq Ahmed Gurmani for Pakistan and Sardar Ibrahim Khan for Azad Kashmir, the agreement reportedly transferred control of Gilgit-Baltistan (then the "Northern Areas") to direct Pakistani administration, along with defense, foreign affairs and communications. Per secondary accounts, it went unreported in 1949 press coverage and unmentioned in contemporary memoirs, only becoming public knowledge in the 1990s.

Secondary sourcing only (Wikipedia, citing later scholarship) — the primary agreement text has not been located or read for this archive.

7 May 1949India's formal rejection — and Bajpai's own secret admission that partition is the answer

The same telegram carries two very different registers: a formal government position, and then Bajpai breaking from it entirely.

Full text read
India rejects the truce terms over Azad forces and the Northern Areas ▾

Bajpai told Henderson India's formal answer to UNCIP's truce proposals "would almost certainly be no," on two specific grounds: India wanted firmer assurances on disarming and disbanding the bulk of Azad forces before agreeing to any withdrawal schedule, and it could not accept UNCIP's position that the Northern Areas (Gilgit-Baltistan) would continue under "local authorities established by and loyal to Pakistan" — noting dryly that India had understood UNCIP's earlier position on the Northern Areas to be different from its position on Azad territory proper, but "in practice difference seemed slight since proposal is apparently that both territories are to be governed by Pakistan puppets." Bajpai expressed genuine regret, expecting the matter would go back to the Security Council "for again acrimonious discussions which would serve no useful purpose," and pre-empted the inevitable charge that India was rejecting the terms to "sabotage plebiscite" — arguing India would not have agreed to a plebiscite at all if it hadn't been willing to hold one, "under conditions which would give" a fair result.

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, 7 May 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, Document 1186 (501.BC Kashmir/5–749).
Full text read
"Partition would be soundest solution" ▾

Then, "on purely personal basis," Bajpai said something he could never put in a formal government position: although India should proceed with a plebiscite if Pakistan insisted, "plebiscite will not solve Kashmir problem... Partition would be soundest solution. Unfortunately Pakistan continues blindly and emotionally to insist on plebiscite and all of Kashmir." He added that he was "not in position [to] suggest partition since Pakistan would construe such suggestion as fear that India would lose plebiscite."

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, 7 May 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, Document 1186 (501.BC Kashmir/5–749).

Read alongside Austin's October 1948 telegram above, this means both governments' most senior officials privately favored partition over a plebiscite at roughly the same time — and neither could say so publicly without the other side reading it as weakness. Days earlier, Washington had also quietly been managing its own most valuable asset for the moment arbitration might become necessary.

Full text read
Protecting Nimitz: reserved "as possible last resort" ▾

Instructing both embassies ahead of a 5 May deadline, the State Department was explicit that "possibility Nimitz acting as arbitrator or otherwise entering truce negotiations shld for present be minimized" — reserved only as "a possible last resort" if UNCIP's own negotiations collapsed entirely, specifically to avoid "protracted delay" placing him in "an untenable position." Nimitz's name was being deliberately kept out of the fight until there was no alternative left.

The Secretary of State to the Embassy in India, 2 May 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, 501.BC Kashmir/5–249.

June–July 1949The Chyle episode

Six months into the ceasefire, Henderson reported something UNCIP's other members found genuinely unsettling — and it turned out to explain a decision this archive had already recorded without knowing why.

Full text read
A Communist-bloc commissioner, secret talks with Sheikh Abdullah, and a Soviet-independence rumor ▾

"Czech member UNCIP, Chyle, has had private talks with Sheikh Abdullah and other members Kashmir Government during past ten days. UNCIP members have no legitimate reason deal with Kashmir Government and he volunteers no information re nature of talks. Curious onlookers within UNCIP speculate that he may be discussing possibility independence Kashmir with backing Soviet bloc." Henderson adds that Chyle was "conceited, aggressive, imperious, impatient of opposition" beneath an outwardly cooperative manner.

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, 9 June 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, Document 1193 (501.BC Kashmir/6–949).

Dr. Oldřich Chyle had succeeded Josef Korbel as Czechoslovakia's UNCIP representative — Korbel's daughter, Madeleine Albright, would become US Secretary of State half a century later. By September 1949, secondary Indian sources report Chyle was directly advising Kashmir state minister D.P. Dhar, who wrote Nehru a "top secret and personal" note after "extensive meetings" with him: an American-proposed arbitrator, Chyle warned, would "lord over" both the Commission and the two governments, reducing Kashmir's own position to "one of abject pity." Chyle separately called the whole arbitration push "a pre-planned attempt on the part of the USA and UK to intervene in the dispute." (This second-hand account of Chyle's private advice is not independently verified against a primary Indian source and should be treated as reported, not confirmed.)

Full text read
Isolated 4-to-1, and Washington's own fear of Soviet obstruction ▾

By 5 July, "all members except Czechoslovak now take same view" that further arbitration negotiation was useless — Chyle stood alone. Three days later, the State Department gave Henderson its own reasoning for avoiding a Security Council referral: it would "provide opportunity Soviet obstruction," citing specific — if unprinted — Moscow-to-Delhi and Moscow-to-Karachi cables Washington was tracking directly.

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, 5 July 1949; The Secretary of State to the Embassy in India, 8 July 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, 501.BC Kashmir/7–549 and /7–849.

15–18 August 1949Nehru, unguarded

Weeks into the arbitration push, Nehru summoned Henderson personally — and gave the rawest, least diplomatic account of his position found anywhere in this archive.

Full text read
"He would not give an inch... even if Kashmir, India and the whole world would go to pieces" ▾

"He said he was tired of receiving moralistic advice from US. India did not need advice from US or any other country as to its foreign or internal policies... So far as Kashmir was concerned he would not give an inch. He would hold his ground even if Kashmir, India and the whole world would go to pieces." Nehru framed the dispute in explicitly civilizational terms: India's secular, pluralist philosophy against what he called Pakistan's "theocratic state" — with Kashmir as the proof of which vision could work.

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, 15 August 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, Document 1205 (845.00/8–1549).

Bajpai told Henderson separately that the State Department's own expressions of concern had been "misinterpreted" as doubting India's good faith — Washington's formal reply, cabled back on 5 August, insisted "the Dept remains convinced that both India and Pak desire a peaceful solution" and regretted the misunderstanding. Nehru's confrontation with Henderson happened regardless, days later.

25–31 August 1949Truman writes to both Prime Ministers

Ten days after Nehru's outburst, Washington played its highest card — a personal letter from the President himself, sent to Nehru and Liaquat Ali Khan simultaneously.

Full text read — the founding document
Truman, in his own words: "I urge you to accept the recommendation for arbitration" ▾

"Asia is today in the midst of a great social and political revolution... In the interest of your own nation and in the interest of the world community, I urge you to accept the recommendation for arbitration of truce terms now placed before your Govt by the UN. I am addressing a similar communication to the Prime Minister of [Pakistan/India]." The instruction to deliver it was explicit: same day as UNCIP's own presentation of the arbitration proposal, or within 24 hours to the Foreign Office if the Prime Minister was unavailable.

The Secretary of State to the Embassy in India (text of President Truman's message), 25 August 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, Document 1206 (501.BC Kashmir/8–2549).

Days later, Acheson personally met Nehru's own sister, Ambassador Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit, to press the point further — tying it explicitly to Nehru's broader standing in Asia.

Full text read
Acheson to Pandit: "We looked to him to assume the leadership in the rehabilitation of Asia" ▾

"In conversations which I had had with the President concerning the situation in China and the Far East it had been apparent to both of us that the Ambassador's brother, the Prime Minister of India, had emerged as a world figure of great influence and that we looked to him to assume the leadership in the rehabilitation of Asia... we deeply hoped that in receiving the President's personal appeal the Prime Minister would give the most careful consideration" to arbitration. Pandit thanked him warmly, praised her brother's record on Kashmir, but committed to nothing.

Memorandum of Conversation by the Secretary of State, 30 August 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, 501.BC Kashmir/8–2949.

8 September 1949Two replies, two very different answers

Pakistan's Prime Minister accepted within hours of receiving Truman's letter. India's did not — though his written reply was careful never to say so outright.

Full text read
Liaquat Ali Khan accepts, and points at India ▾

Signed the same evening the letter arrived, delivered urgently and in secret: "I thank you for your message of August 30, 1949, urging the acceptance of the recommendation... The Pakistan Government greatly appreciates the keen interest you have shown to the early settlement of the Kashmir dispute... India has hitherto resisted every effort made by Security Council and later on by the United Nations Commission to bring about that result."

The Chargé in Pakistan (Wolf) to the Secretary of State, transmitting Liaquat's letter, 8 September 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, Document 1210.
Full text read
Nehru replies warmly — and commits to nothing ▾

"My dear Mr. President, I thank you for your letter of the 30th August... In view of the importance of its subject matter, I had to consult my colleagues and the Governments of Jammu and Kashmir. This will explain the slight delay... I greatly appreciate the friendly solicitude which prompted you to write and particularly welcome your frankness because it enables me to write with equal candour." The reply, forwarded early by Bajpai so Washington would "know nature reply in advance," was friendly in tone and technically left the door open — but Bajpai told Henderson separately that he did not believe further arbitration attempts would help, and hoped UNCIP could "be wound up soon."

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, transmitting Nehru's reply, 8 September 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, Document 1209 (501.BC Kashmir/9–849).

Read together, these two replies are the cleanest evidence in the whole archive of how differently Pakistan and India handled the same American pressure: Pakistan said yes in writing and used the moment to needle India publicly; India said nothing that could be quoted against it, and let the substance die quietly through Bajpai instead.

December 1949A third mediator, and Nehru's four options

With UNCIP's own efforts exhausted, a new track opened before the year was out — General A.G.L. McNaughton of Canada, working under a mid-December Security Council resolution, meeting separately with India's and Pakistan's representatives to find revised common ground. Nehru rejected his proposals too. Days later, Britain's High Commissioner, General Nye, pressed Nehru directly with a stark framing.

Full text read
Nye to Nehru: war, stalemate, mediation, or arbitration — pick one ▾

Nye "took line with Prime Minister that there were only four possibilities: war, continued stalemate, mediation and arbitration. As first two were unthinkable it remained to discuss latter two." He pointed out that Nehru's own rejection of McNaughton's mediation proposals was itself evidence India wasn't prepared to compromise — so arbitration was the only path left, if India genuinely wanted a settlement. The record is blunt about the result: "Nehru apparently gave Nye no encouragement whatever." The principal Indian decision-makers behind the rejection were named as Nehru, Governor-General Patel, and Kashmir's Deputy Prime Minister, Bakshi Ghulam Mohammad.

FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, ~p. 1770 (exact document number not yet confirmed).
Full text read
India's revised position, relayed after McNaughton's talks with Bajpai ▾

Austin transmits "the text of Indian position as revised in light of McNaughton's conversation with Bajpai this afternoon" — a formal restatement of India's stance following the Council's 17 December resolution authorizing McNaughton to consult both parties and report back.

The US Representative at the UN (Austin) to the Secretary of State, 19 December 1949. FRUS 1949, Vol. VI, 501.BC Kashmir/12–1949.

April–September 1950The Dixon mission

With McNaughton's effort also dead, the Security Council appointed Sir Owen Dixon, an Australian jurist, as UN Representative on 12 April 1950 — a role carrying most of UNCIP's own powers. Dixon deliberately avoided negotiating with each Prime Minister separately.

Full text read
Dixon's method: make them talk to each other, in his presence ▾

Bajpai told Henderson that Dixon "believes it would be mistake for him to talk to each Prime Minister separately re matter demilitarization. His plan is to persuade them talk to each other in his presence on subject. If they fail find basis for agreement he might then offer suggestions."

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, 2 June 1950. FRUS 1950, Vol. V, Document 794 (357.AB/6–250).

By July, Dixon had gone further than any previous mediator — proposing what became known as "regional plebiscites": voting only in the genuinely contested Kashmir Valley, while allocating other areas without a vote on the assumption their outcome was already predictable. What made this conceivable at all was a decision India's own Cabinet had just taken, in secret.

Full text read — TOP SECRET
India's Cabinet privately prepares to lose the Vale ▾

"Cabinet prepared now abandon idea of Vale going to India provided Jammu and Ladakh would be retained and decision re Vale would be based on plebiscite" — a plebiscite the Cabinet itself expected would hand the Valley to Pakistan. Nehru, "somewhat reluctant," went along with the Cabinet's position, but insisted on treating the whole approach "with reserve" so that Liaquat would not read it as India making "a great concession." The cable notes Sheikh Abdullah and his own associates were expected to "protest loudly" if the arrangement ever became real — Kashmir's own government was no part of the calculation.

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, 29 July 1950. FRUS 1950, Vol. V, Document 797 (357.AB/7–2950).

Getting from that private Cabinet position to an actual conference required real maneuvering neither side wanted seen in public — and when it finally reached the table, Pakistan's own fallback made clear a fair vote was never really the point for either side.

Full text read
Nehru wants deniability; Liaquat refuses once he learns what's on the table ▾

"Nehru had refused allow Dixon make offer to Liaquat on his behalf. He had however, agreed that Dixon could outline to Liaquat what Nehru had said." Dixon planned to privately warn Liaquat he might propose "partition plus plebiscite" — but "Liaquat and his associates flatly refused attend conference for purpose discussing partition plus plebiscite" once that became clear. Nehru, told his own idea had collapsed the conference before it started, wondered aloud whether Dixon should simply return to Lake Success rather than report the mission a failure outright. When the conference seemed to be failing, Liaquat offered his own alternative: partition with no plebiscite at all, the Vale going to Pakistan outright — which Dixon rejected as "out of question."

Ambassador Henderson to the Secretary of State, 11 August 1950. FRUS 1950, Vol. V, Document 799 (357.AB/8–1150).

Dixon reported to the Security Council on 15 September 1950 that no agreement had been reached on demilitarization or a plebiscite, and formally requested that his own position be terminated. Ten days later, in a private dinner conversation not meant for the record, he said what he'd never put in writing.

Full text read
Off the record: "He believed that the Vale of Kashmir should go to Pakistan" ▾

Dixon told Assistant Secretary Hickerson privately that "the Vale of Kashmir should go to Pakistan," and floated a trade: since securing India's agreement to a fair plebiscite in the Valley was "so exceedingly difficult," Pakistan might simply be given the Valley outright, in exchange for concessions to India elsewhere. He separately judged that Kashmir was being "kept alive by the Indians as an issue on which to hang other aims" — including, he suggested, an excuse for maintaining a large standing army.

Memorandum of Conversation by the Assistant Secretary of State for UN Affairs (Hickerson), 25 September 1950. FRUS 1950, Vol. V, Document 808 (357.AB/9–2550).

The UN's own chief mediator, in other words, had a settlement he privately believed was fair — and a theory of why India wouldn't accept any settlement at all — that never appeared in his official report. By that autumn, London and Washington had already begun quietly discussing a new joint Security Council initiative to follow Dixon's failure.

1950–1952It never really died — a pattern of appeals, and Nehru's own case

Arbitration kept resurfacing for years after Truman's letter, and so did a specific pattern: Pakistan's Foreign Minister, Sir Zafrullah Khan, returning again and again to personally press whoever was Secretary of State, across three separate years.

Full text read
Zafrullah, "almost desperately," to Acheson — twice ▾

On 6 January 1950, Zafrullah told Acheson that McNaughton's mediation had also failed — India's amendments to his proposals amounted, in substance, to rejection — and that he had come "almost desperately" seeking advice. Ten months later, after Dixon's mission had also collapsed, he was back: recalling that Acheson had personally predicted Dixon's "personal qualities" would prove equal to the task, and noting, with what reads like genuine fatigue, that despite those qualities "the Kashmir case was again before the Security Council." The same 90-minute meeting also covered Afghanistan, Nepal, Tibet and Korea — a reminder of how much else was competing for Acheson's attention by late 1950.

Memorandum of Conversation by the Secretary of State, 6 January 1950 (FRUS 1950, Vol. V, Document 759) and 17 November 1950 (Document 810).

Britain, watching the same cycle of failed mediators, had already made a quiet prediction about how it would actually end.

Cited, not read
A British official predicts the ceasefire line will simply become the border ▾

In a US-UK discussion that autumn, McGhee and a British official reportedly discussed separating "demilitarization" (the Security Council's own responsibility) from "territorial aspects" (left to the two parties themselves) — with the British side predicting that, in practice, this separation would mean the existing ceasefire line simply becoming the permanent frontier. It is, more or less, what happened over the following decades.

Referenced in State Department records, autumn 1950; full document not yet independently verified.

Arbitration itself kept resurfacing. In July 1951, Acheson personally briefed Truman on Kashmir again — Truman "expressed his concern over the trend of affairs in India," with Australia's Prime Minister Menzies also engaging the White House directly. Frank Graham succeeded Dixon as UN Representative, and by October 1951 was deliberately holding back a dramatic report to the Council, telling State Department officials he judged "sometimes more boldness was required to proceed slowly." By September, his own twelve-point plan still included — as its thirteenth clause — a fresh arbitration proposal, two years after Nehru had quietly let the first one die. Two more Zafrullah meetings followed in 1951, one with McGhee explicitly denying US favoritism when Zafrullah challenged him on it directly.

In February 1952, Truman personally approved sending Graham back for yet another attempt. That July, Nehru gave the fullest public statement of his Kashmir position found anywhere in this archive.

Full text read
Nehru: accession "complete in law and in fact" — but the pledge survives anyway ▾

"While the accession was complete in law and in fact, the other fact, which has nothing to do with law also remains, namely our pledge... that this matter can be reaffirmed or cancelled or cut out by the people of Kashmir, if they so wish" — "no forced marriages, no forced unions." On why India rejected UN arbitration: it would have "placed the aggressor and the aggressed on an equal footing."

Statement to the House of the People, 24 July 1952. Selected Works of Jawaharlal Nehru, Second Series, Vol. 19.

1961–1963

Kennedy

The one administration in this whole archive where a president personally, repeatedly pushed for a Kashmir settlement — not through subordinates, but in his own signed directive and his own envoy's pressure on Nehru.

22–23 June 1962Nehru's raw reaction to a Security Council vote

The Council's Kashmir debate concluded 22 June 1962 with a resolution urging direct negotiations; the US voted in favour, but it failed on a Soviet veto. The next day, in the Indian Parliament, Nehru reacted with unusual rawness.

Full text read
Nehru: "deep regret and sorrow" at the US and UK ▾

Nehru told Parliament he felt "deep regret and sorrow" that the US and UK "should almost invariably be against us" on Goa and Kashmir alike, saying the debate had "hurt and injured" India and created "doubt in our minds about the goodwill" of both powers.

FRUS 1961–63, Vol. XIX, Editorial Note, Document 145.

6 December 1961Kennedy's own directive

Months before that vote, Kennedy had personally signed a directive that named a Kashmir settlement as an explicit US objective — not a hoped-for byproduct of other policy, but a stated goal.

Full text read
NSAM 209: "a plan of action to encourage a Kashmir settlement" ▾

Kennedy's own National Security Action Memorandum approves emergency military aid to India and directs the NSC's South Asia Subcommittee to prepare recommendations including, explicitly, "a plan of action to encourage a Kashmir settlement." Copies went to the CIA Director and the Bureau of the Budget.

National Security Action Memorandum No. 209, FRUS 1961–63, Vol. XIX, Document 223.

22–28 November 1962Harriman presses Nehru, after the China war

The single richest document found across this entire project. Days after India's ceasefire with China, Averell Harriman met Nehru repeatedly to press for a Kashmir settlement, reported by Ambassador Galbraith in extraordinary detail.

Full text read
Nehru, in his own words, rules options in and out ▾

Nehru explicitly rejected both "giving half to Pakistan" and "independent status for Kashmir," while suggesting arrangements along lines proposed by M.J. Desai and Galbraith himself might work. By the third meeting, he had agreed in principle to ministerial-level talks with Ayub — though his opening position would be limited to "adjustment [of the] cease fire line."

Telegram From the Embassy in India (Galbraith), FRUS 1961–63, Vol. XIX, Document 214.

Two further State Department documents from this period — an instruction to reassure Ayub of "US determination to push for Kashmir settlement," and an internal assessment that no US initiative was yet possible "at this stage" — round out a period in which Kashmir had genuine, sustained White House attention.

November–December 1963An impasse report, drafted across a transition

A 14-page CIA Office of Current Intelligence report lays out the dispute's history since 1846 and a precise order of battle — roughly 25,000 Pakistani/Azad Kashmir troops against roughly 45,000 Indian Army troops. Internal references to "incidents in late October" as very recent place its drafting in the November-December 1963 window — meaning it was very likely written across Kennedy's assassination, and its true authorship period cannot be cleanly assigned to either president.

13–20 September 1965A dedicated Kashmir Task Force

During the 1965 war itself, a CIA internal activity report confirms the Agency stood up dedicated organizational machinery for the crisis.

Full text read
"Drafted full time for service on the Kashmir Task Force" ▾

The report records the week's publication of "CIA/BI GM 66-2, Kashmir" — a geographic intelligence memorandum — and states plainly that its author had been assigned full-time to a dedicated Kashmir Task Force during the war.

1964–1968The nuclear-anxiety correspondence

A 22-document collection of State Department nuclear-policy material — abstracted, not yet individually read in full — runs from 1961 to 1983, but its Johnson-era highlights are substantial: an October 1964 JCS memo (after China's first nuclear test) opposing security guarantees to India that might alienate Pakistan, and a January 1967 JCS memo predicting India would "probably" test a device "within the next few years." Neither is about Kashmir territorially, but both tie India's China-driven nuclear anxiety, and Pakistan's alliance with Washington, directly back to the unresolved dispute.

Sources: CIA Reading Room; National Security Archive Briefing Book No. 6; FRUS 1964-68 Vol. XXV (cited, not yet read in full). 3 documents currently in this section; the 1965 war's own FRUS volume is the clear next target.

Nixon

28 March 1971The Blood Telegram

Consul General Archer Blood's cable to Washington, provided directly by a reader of this archive after the original scan proved unreachable through normal research channels.

Full text read
"Selective Genocide" — and a direct challenge to US policy ▾

"Here in Dacca we are mute and horrified witnesses to a reign of terror by the Pak military," Blood opens, naming specific murdered university faculty and reporting most elected Assembly members were also targeted. He directly questions whether Washington should keep "pretending to believe GOP false assertions and denying" the atrocities — and weighs his own likely expulsion against the value of keeping the reporting channel open.

Department of State Telegram, Dacca to SecState Washington, Cable DACCA 0959. Note: the transmission date-time group reads 280540Z MAR 71 — 28 March, not the 27 March date commonly cited in secondary accounts.

1971Kissinger's own office, mid-crisis

A TOP SECRET/SENSITIVE CODEWORD meeting record from Kissinger's own office files captures Assistant Secretary Sisco, Under Secretary Johnson, and CIA Director Helms arguing over Chinese troop posture.

Substantial excerpt
"Enough forces there to rattle the sword" ▾

Sisco presses whether Chinese activity concerns "that strip of Azad Kashmir" specifically; Helms responds China already had "enough forces there to rattle the sword." The same record discusses holding Jordan's King Hussein back from supplying aircraft to Pakistan pending a presidential decision.

Kissinger office files, "South Asia," LOC-HAK-556-8-5-1 (declassified in part, 2011).
